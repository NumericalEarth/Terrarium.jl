Soil hydrology

Overview

Soil hydrology processes characterize the dynamics of ground water in both saturated and unsaturated soil. It defines parameters and methods needed to compute water fluxes between layers and grid cells within the soil domain. Implementations should extend AbstractSoilHydrology and should generally consist of at least four components:

  • A scheme for computing vertical water fluxes between soil layers
  • A closure parameterization linking soil saturation and pressure head
  • A parameterization for soil hydraulic properties
  • A forcing term representing user-defined, internal sources/sinks in the soil domain (not including evapotranspiration)

Static soil hydrology ("No Flow")

The simplest possible soil hydrology scheme is one in which the soil saturation state remains constant over time. This can be appropriate for simulations in regions where the soil is normally waterlogged or where changes in the hydrological state can be otherwise assumed to play a negligible role.

Richardson-Richards equation for variably saturated flow

The vertical flow of water in porous media, such as soils, can be formulated as following the conservation law

\[ \phi\frac{\partial \xi(\psi)}{\partial t} = - \boldsymbol{\nabla} \cdot \textbf{j}_{\text{w}} + F_{\text{w}}(z,t),\]

where (as defined in Soil stratigraphy) $\phi$ is the natural porosity (or saturated water content) of the soil volume and $\xi \in [0,1]$ the saturation of pore water/ice. $F_{\text{w}}(z,t)$ is an inhomogeneous source/sink (forcing) term (1/s) and $\textbf{j}_{\text{w}}$ the water flux vector (m/s).

Vertical fluxes in the soil column can be represented by combining gravity-driven advection with Darcy's law

\[\begin{equation} \textbf{j}_{\text{w}}^{\mathrm{v}} =\textbf{j}_{\text{w}} \cdot \mathbf{n}_z = -\kappa_{\text{w}}\frac{\partial \left(\psi + z\right)}{\partial z}, \end{equation}\]

where $\psi$ is the matric potential (m), $\mathbf{n}_z$ the normal vector perpendicular to the surface and $\kappa_{\text{w}}$ the hydraulic conductivity (m/s). Given the positive upwards convention (see Numerical core), the $z-$axis is thus defined positive away from the surface. Substituting this equation into the aforementioned conservation law yields the widely known Richardson-Richards equation for variably saturated flow in porous media [7]. When the divergence of the water flux vector ($\boldsymbol{\nabla} \cdot \textbf{j}_{\text{w}}$) is simplified to only consider vertical water fluxes, this gives:

\[\begin{equation} \phi\frac{\partial \xi(\psi)}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left[\kappa_w \frac{\partial \left(\psi + z\right)}{\partial z}\right] + F_{\text{w}}(z,t) \end{equation}\]

which is called the mixed-form equation, as the rate of change on the left-hand side is in $\xi$ but the vertical gradient on the right-hand side is in $\psi$ [2].

The Timestepping trait argument of compute_tendencies! for RichardsEq is the resolved timestepping class of saturation_water_ice (see Implicit-explicit time stepping). source
  • Under Explicit(), the tendency contains the full divergence of the darcy_flux, the evapotranspiration sink, the user-defined forcing, and the flux boundary conditions on saturation_water_ice (e.g. InfiltrationFlux).
  • Under Implicit(), the Darcy fluxes through the interior cell faces are omitted, since they are integrated by the implicit sub-stepper. Only the Darcy fluxes through the two boundary faces of the column remain, via compute_boundary_darcy_flux_divergence. These vanish for the default (no-flux) conditions on pressure_head and carry the prescribed flux for a gradient condition on pressure_head (e.g. FreeDrainage).

In both cases, the Darcy fluxes at $t^n$ through all cell faces are stored in the water_flux auxiliary variable (m/s, positive upward, on cell faces; see compute_water_flux). Under Implicit(), the implicit step overwrites the fluxes through the interior faces with those of its conservative update (compute_implicit_water_flux). After a step, water_flux therefore holds the flux that moved the water, with which the soil energy advects heat (see Soil energy balance).

The hydrology closure redistributes over- and undersaturation with adjust_saturation_profile!. It moves the sensible heat $C_\text{w} T$ of the water it shifts between cells in the internal_energy, at the temperature of the cell that the water leaves, and removes that of the excess water it discards (standalone SoilModel) or routes to the surface_excess_water pool (LandModel), which stores no heat. The energy closure, which runs after the hydrology closure, then recomputes the temperature from the adjusted energy.

The implicit step solves the mixed form of the Richards equation (see Richardson-Richards equation for variably saturated flow) with the mass-conservative modified Picard iteration of [3], using the lagged face conductivity of the Darcy flux and the lagged specific moisture capacity given by compute_implicit_conductivity! and compute_implicit_capacity!. With porosity $\phi$, saturation $\xi$ (so that the volumetric water content is $\theta = \phi \xi$), and total hydraulic head $\Psi = \psi_m(\theta) + \psi_z + \psi_h$ (matric, elevation, and hydrostatic head), the backward Euler step reads

\[\phi\, \frac{\xi^{n+1} - \xi^n}{\Delta t} = \frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left[K \frac{\partial \Psi^{n+1}}{\partial z}\right] + \phi\, G_\text{exp}\,,\]

where $G_\text{exp}$ (1/s) is the explicit tendency of the saturation $\xi$; it is multiplied by $\phi$ because the equation is written for the volumetric water content $\theta = \phi \xi$, the quantity whose flux divergence is the right-hand side. $G_\text{exp}$ contains the flux boundary conditions on the saturation (e.g. InfiltrationFlux), the Darcy fluxes through boundary faces implied by gradient conditions on the pressure head, evapotranspiration, and the user-defined forcing. The elevation head and the hydrostatic head below the water table are held fixed within the step, so that $\partial\theta/\partial\Psi = C$, the specific moisture capacity. Linearizing the water content about the latest iterate $m$, $\phi \xi^{m+1} \approx \phi \xi^m + C^m (\Psi^{m+1} - \Psi^m)$, with the hydraulic conductivity $K^m$ of the Darcy flux lagged at the same iterate, gives the tridiagonal system

\[C^m\, \Psi^{m+1} - \Delta t\, \frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left[K^m \frac{\partial \Psi^{m+1}}{\partial z}\right] = C^m\, \Psi^m - \phi\,(\xi^m - \xi^+)\,, \qquad \xi^+ = \xi^n + \Delta t\, G_\text{exp}\,,\]

for the total head, starting from $\xi^0 = \xi^n$. As for heat conduction (see Implicit vertical heat conduction), the saturation is then updated conservatively from the implicit fluxes through the interior faces,

\[\xi^{m+1} = \xi^+ + \frac{\Delta t}{\phi}\, \frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left[K^m \frac{\partial \Psi^{m+1}}{\partial z}\right],\]

and the hydrology closure is applied after every iteration: it redistributes over- and undersaturation (see adjust_saturation_profile!), updates the water table, and recomputes $\Psi^{m+1}$ from $\xi^{m+1}$. Excess water that reaches a saturated surface is routed into the surface_excess_water pool of a LandModel's surface runoff only by the closure of the last iteration (and is discarded by a standalone SoilModel), since every iteration restarts from $\xi^+$. For the same reason, only the closure of the last iteration moves the heat of the redistributed water (see Coupling of water and energy), so the internal energy is adjusted exactly once per step. The column water content therefore changes by exactly $\Delta t$ times the boundary fluxes and sources in $G_\text{exp}$, minus the excess routed to the surface pool, for any number of iterations.

In saturated cells $C = 0$, and the diagonal of the system is carried by the conductivity terms $\Delta t K / \Delta z^2$. Where the conductivity vanishes as well (frozen saturated cells), the system would be singular, so $C$ is bounded from below by the small minimum_moisture_capacity (1/m, default $10^{-5}$) of the hydraulic properties (see ConstantSoilHydraulics), which acts like a small specific storage within the iteration but does not affect mass conservation. [3] recommend at least two iterations, which is the default of Picard.

Flux boundary conditions on saturation_water_ice and gradient boundary conditions on pressure_head are supported; value boundary conditions on pressure_head are not yet supported.

The terms that stay explicit can remove more water from a cell in one step than it holds, e.g. gravity drainage through the bottom face of a draining sandy column. water_outflow_timescale reports the time in which they would drain a cell to its residual saturation, which bounds the step of a TimeStepWizard through cell_advection_timescale (see Adaptive time stepping with IMEX). It evaluates the explicit terms selected by the resolved class of saturation_water_ice, so under Explicit() it includes the interior Darcy fluxes as well.

The implicit operator of saturation_water_ice is a TridiagonalOperator in the total hydraulic head pressure_head, the variable of the SoilSaturationPressureClosure (see its varname method below), returned by implicit_operator (which also validates the configuration), and each iteration of the implicit solver calls the process hooks of SoilHydrology with RichardsEq below:

Terrarium.implicit_operator — Method
implicit_operator(
    hydrology::SoilHydrology{NF, RichardsEq, SaturationClosure, SoilHydraulics} where {SaturationClosure<:Terrarium.AbstractSoilWaterClosure, SoilHydraulics<:(Terrarium.AbstractSoilHydraulics{NF})},
    state,
    grid
) where NF

Allocate the TridiagonalOperator of saturation_water_ice on ground_domain(grid). Throws an ArgumentError unless the closure is the SoilSaturationPressureClosure and pressure_head has no value boundary conditions.

The operator is the backward Euler step of the Richards equation in the total pressure head Ψ, after the mass-conservative modified Picard iteration of [3]: with porosity φ, saturation ξ, and ξ⁺ = ξⁿ + Δt G_exp from the explicit stage, the water content is linearized about the iterate m, φ ξᵐ⁺¹ ≈ φ ξᵐ + Cᵐ (Ψᵐ⁺¹ - Ψᵐ) with the specific moisture capacity Cᵐ and the hydraulic conductivity Kᵐ lagged, giving the tridiagonal system Cᵐ Ψᵐ⁺¹ - Δt ∂z(Kᵐ ∂z Ψᵐ⁺¹) = Cᵐ Ψᵐ - φ (ξᵐ - ξ⁺) (see the soil hydrology docs for the derivation). Each Picard iteration m = 0, …, M-1, starting from ξ⁰ = ξⁿ, calls the hooks:

  1. linearize!: hydraulic conductivity, lagged Kᵐ (faces) and Cᵐ (centers, bounded below by minimum_moisture_capacity so that frozen saturated cells stay regular), and the right-hand side;
  2. solve!: the system above for Ψᵐ⁺¹;
  3. implicit_update!: ξᵐ⁺¹ = ξ⁺ + Δt ∂z(Kᵐ ∂z Ψᵐ⁺¹) / φ through the interior faces, which conserves water exactly, and water_flux ← -Kᵐ ∂z Ψᵐ⁺¹, the flux with which the heat step advects heat;
  4. implicit_closure!: redistribution of over- and undersaturation, water table, Ψᵐ⁺¹, and hydraulic conductivity. Since every iteration restarts from ξ⁺, only the final one routes surface excess water to the runoff pool and moves the heat of redistributed water.

The solver calls the hooks through the SoilModel/LandModel forwarding methods and the SoilEnergyWaterCarbon methods in soil_coupled.jl.

Hydraulic properties

Match the conductivity to the retention curve

The unsaturated hydraulic conductivity and the soil water retention curve should come from the same family. By default, ConstantSoilHydraulics and SoilHydraulicsSURFEX select the conductivity that matches the retention curve (see Terrarium.default_unsat_hydraulic_cond). In particular, UnsatKLinear is not suitable for dry soil with a Brooks–Corey retention curve: the matric potential grows as a power law of the inverse saturation while the conductivity falls only linearly, so that the suction fluxes in dry soil become unphysically large.

Besides the static soil characteristics mentioned in Soil stratigraphy such as porosity ($\phi$), a key time-varying hydraulic property is the (non-saturated) hydraulic conductivity $\kappa_{\text{w}}$. Different ways of modelling $\kappa_{\text{w}}$ are defined as subyptes of AbstractUnsatK. The simplest formulation, called UnsatKLinear, models $\kappa_{\text{w}}$ as:

\[\begin{equation} \kappa_{\text{w}} = \frac{\theta_{\text{liq}}}{\theta + \theta_{\text{air}}} \kappa_{\text{w,sat}}\, \end{equation}\]

with $\kappa_{\text{w,sat}}$ the saturated hydraulic conductivity (m/s). A more complex formulation taking ice into account, called UnsatKVanGenuchten,follows [8], here presented in the form of [9, Eq. (26)]:

\[\begin{equation} \kappa_{\text{w}} = I_\text{ice}\left(\frac{\theta_{\text{liq}}}{\phi}\right)^{0.5} \left[1 - \left(1 - \left(\frac{\theta_{\text{liq}}}{\phi}\right)^{n/(n+1)}\right)^{(n-1)/n}\right]^2 \kappa_{\text{w,sat}}\,, \end{equation}\]

which adds an ice impedance factor $I_\text{ice} = 10^{-\Omega\theta_{\text{ice}}/\theta}$ to the classic van Genuchten formulation to account for the blocking of water-filled pores by ice.

The conductivity of the Brooks–Corey model, called UnsatKBrooksCorey, is a power law of the liquid water saturation, with the same ice impedance factor:

\[\begin{equation} \kappa_{\text{w}} = I_\text{ice}\left(\frac{\theta_{\text{liq}}}{\phi}\right)^{3 + 2/\lambda} \kappa_{\text{w,sat}}\,, \end{equation}\]

where $\lambda$ is the pore size distribution index of the retention curve. [10] derived the exponent from their retention curve using the pore size distribution model of Burdine; [11] obtained the same law, written with $b = 1/\lambda$ as $(\theta_{\text{liq}}/\phi)^{2b + 3}$, and [12] tabulated $b$ by soil texture. In this form, it is the conductivity used in SURFEX/ISBA [13].

Terrarium.UnsatKBrooksCorey — Type
struct UnsatKBrooksCorey{NF} <: Terrarium.AbstractUnsatK{NF}

Unsaturated hydraulic conductivity of the Brooks–Corey model, extended with an ice impedance factor:

K = I_ice K_sat S^(3 + 2/λ)

where S = θw / θsat is the liquid water saturation, λ is the pore size distribution index of the BrooksCorey retention curve, and I_ice is the Terrarium.ice_impedance_factor of [9].

Brooks and Corey [10] define the power law on the effective saturation (θw − θres) / (θsat − θres). Terrarium evaluates the retention curve with the residual water content of the BrooksCorey curve itself, which is zero by default, and in that case the effective saturation reduces to S. A nonzero residual water content of the retention curve is not yet accounted for here; the residual_saturation of the hydraulic properties is only a numerical lower bound on the saturation.

The power law and its exponent 3 + 2/λ were derived by [10] from their retention curve, using the pore size distribution model of Burdine. [11] obtained the same law, written as S^(2b + 3) with b = 1/λ, and [12] tabulated b by soil texture; in this form, it is the conductivity of SURFEX/ISBA [13]. The exponent λ is taken from the retention curve so that the two cannot be configured inconsistently; this formulation can therefore only be combined with a BrooksCorey retention curve.

Closures

Critical to the modelling of water transport in the soil, is the relationship between soil water/ice saturation ($\xi$) and the matric potential ($\Psi_m$), which is called the soil-water retention curve (SWRC). Recall from the documentation on the core interfaces that the total hydraulic head $\Psi$ is defined as:

\[\begin{equation} \Psi = \Psi_m(\xi) + \Psi_z + \Psi_h \end{equation}\]

with $\Psi_z$ the elevation head and $\Psi_h$ the hydrostatic head contributed by free water above the water table. Currently, two implementations of the SWRC are available in Terrarium via FreezeCurves.jl: the Brooks-Corey (BrooksCorey) and van Genuchten (VanGenuchten) formulations.

Kernel function that adjusts saturation profiles to account for oversaturation and undersaturation arising due to numerical error. This implementation scans over the saturation profiles at each lateral grid cell and redistributes excess water upward layer-by-layer until reaching the topmost layer, and saturation deficits below the residual saturation downward and then back upward, drawing on the water of the neighboring layers. Water is conserved unless the column as a whole holds less water than its residual saturation, in which case the topmost layer is filled up to the residual saturation. The remaining surface excess is returned as the excess saturation of the topmost layer times its thickness (m); the caller multiplies by the topmost porosity to get a water depth (m³/m²) to route into a surface water pool, or discards it. With an energy process, every saturation change also changes the internal energy in out by the sensible heat of the water (compute_redistributed_heat!).
